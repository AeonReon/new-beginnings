Screens, Done Well

If there's going to be a screen — the honest way to do it.

The real work of these years is play, talk, movement and the outdoors — not a screen. But most children end up in front of one sometimes, so this is the honest position: which few tools genuinely teach, what's gentle enough to watch, and — more than either — how a screen is used, which matters more than most people realise.

🔎 The honest position on screens ▾

Most of what a young child needs comes from play, talk, movement and the real world — not a screen. Bright, fast, rewarding apps and videos train a child to want exactly that: more bright, more fast, more reward. That's the overstimulation problem, and it's real.

But there's a narrow, honest exception. A little calm, focused academic practice — letters, numbers, phonics — can genuinely help, if the tool is well-made and the device isn't feeding the dopamine hit.

That's why the recommendation here is an E-ink tablet for apps, and a projector on the wall for watching. Both do the same thing: take the glossy, close, agitating glow out of it. The child gets the good part without the overstimulation. Same content, a very different effect on a small nervous system.

Programmes like Alpha School (and the wider "future of education" world) show the model worth stealing: a short daily block of focused, adaptive academic work — often well under two hours — that frees the rest of the day for real life, projects and play. The lesson isn't "more screen"; it's that a small, sharp dose of the right tool does the job quickly, so the screen serves the childhood instead of eating it.

📱 Tools that genuinely teach

A short, growing list of the few that earn their screen time — more added as we test them.

📵 Use an E-ink tablet, not a glossy one

A little calm academic practice can genuinely help — but the device matters more than the app. A matte, paper-like E-ink tablet has no glow, no glare and none of the pull of a bright backlit screen, so a child gets the letters-and-numbers practice without the overstimulation. Same app, a very different effect on a small nervous system — and the single biggest lever for keeping screen time on the right side of the line.

Using it well

📵
E-ink over glossy — matte and paper-like beats bright and backlit, every time. The single biggest lever.
⏳
Short and time-boxed — a small daily block with a clear start and stop, not "just one more".
🧑‍🍼
An adult nearby — a tool you use together, not a babysitter you hand over.
🌳
Screen serves the day — a quick academic win so there's more time for the real world, not less.
📺 If they're going to watch

Less is better and none is fine — but if they do watch, what and how matter most.

📽️ Put it on the wall, not in their hands

If they're watching, use a projector on a wall — not a tablet in their hands. Up close on a glossy screen a child's eyes stay locked at one short distance in a pool of bright light — the fixed near-focus tied to short-sightedness, and the glare winds them up. On a big, distant wall their eyes range and refocus the way eyes are meant to; they can stand back, move, even play while it's on; and everyone watches together, facing the same way. Same show, a completely different thing for a small body.

Gentle picks to start — slow, kind, not toxic.

The rules that make it fine

🧱
On the wall, not in the hand — a projector beats a tablet on every count that matters.
🐢
Slow over fast — gentle pacing settles a child; frantic, flashy shows wind them up.
⏳
Short and chosen — a picked thing with an end, not an endless autoplay feed.
👨‍👩‍👧
Together — watched with someone, talked about after. That turns watching into something shared.
